\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Intermittent-demand methods and count models.} Series with many zero periods have been treated
as intermittent demand since \citet{croston1972}, who smoothed the demand size and the interval between
demands separately; the bias correction of \citet{syntetos2005} and the probability-updating variant of
\citet{teunter2011}, which smooths the occurrence indicator on every period, are the methods a reader in the
field looks for first. \citet{shenstone2005} showed that no stochastic model consistent with real
intermittent data underlies Croston's method, so the Croston line gives point forecasts without a predictive
law of its own. Full laws come from count models: \citet{snyder2012} compared a Poisson benchmark, a negative
binomial and a hurdle shifted Poisson that keeps Croston's Bernoulli occurrence, all with dynamic parameters;
\citet{svetunkov2023} wrote occurrence and size as one state space model, iETS, whose occurrence equations
contain TSB's and Croston's as cases; temporal aggregation \citep{nikolopoulos2011}, Tweedie laws
\citep{damato2025} and benchmark suites of distributional methods \citep{damato2026, bai2026} extend the line.
The registered baselines of this paper are classical members of it: the count hurdle is a hurdle shifted
Poisson with a fixed-window occurrence estimate, the empirical hurdle the same occurrence with the empirical
law of the recent positive days, and the dynamic count a discounted Bayesian analogue of TSB. Croston and TSB
were excluded at registration because they carry no predictive law of their own, the dynamic count was
registered to play TSB's part, and a post-hoc descriptive benchmark now places a TSB hurdle beside the
registered forecasters.

\paragraph{Count scores and quantile scores on discrete data.} \citet{czado2009} is the standard reference
for assessing count forecasts by proper scoring rules and the randomized probability integral transform;
\citet{kolassa2016} argued that low retail counts need full discrete laws and scores that respect them;
\citet{wang2024} scored intermittent-demand combinations with the log score, the discrete ranked probability
score and a Brier score. Quantile scores are proper for the quantiles they read \citep{gneiting2007} and equal
a weighted interval score at matching levels \citep{bracher2021}; the M5 uncertainty competition asked for
nine levels from 0.005 to 0.995 \citep{makridakis2022}, and \citet{ziel2022} argued that its leading methods
failed the data's overdispersion and frequent zeros. Two recent evaluations drop every quantile level below the median because the forecasts there are zero for every method \citep{damato2025, damato2026, tichy2025}. The property this paper meets on its very sparse series follows from the definition: a
quantile score reads only the scored quantiles, so two forecasts whose deciles agree score the same for every
target, and a count law with at least nine tenths of its mass at zero has all nine deciles at zero. \qs{},
the registered primary, has that property; the occurrence Brier score and the discrete CRPS, the declared
secondaries, do not.

\paragraph{Foundation models on intermittent data.} Chronos \citep{ansari2024, chronos2-2025}, TimesFM
\citep{das2023} and Moirai \citep{woo2024} are pretrained forecasters run here frozen; Moirai's output is a
mixture with a negative-binomial component, where Chronos-2 and TimesFM expose quantiles, neither family's
paper reports a count analysis, and the general benchmarks name no intermittent category \citep{aksu2024,
fevbench2026}. The evaluations of frozen models on sparse counts are recent: an event-ranking evaluation of
twelve models on five sparse panels without a proper score \citep{schoess2026}; a benchmark of 38 methods
with Croston, SBA, TSB and ADIDA beside Chronos and TimesFM under point and service measures
\citep{chin2026}; TimesFM and Chronos-Bolt against Croston and TSB on quick-commerce demand \citep{kim2026};
TimesFM 3 on simulated intermittent counts under a protocol frozen before any forecast \citep{ebrahim2026,
ebrahim2026zenodo}; and calibration, blind-spot and trained-hurdle studies \citep{adler2025,
ghoroghchian2026, musat2026}. None wraps a frozen model's output in a hurdle under a proper score or asks
whether the model's own zero mass or a context estimate is the better occurrence forecast. Aggregate and
per-series rankings of zero-shot models are known to disagree \citep{islam2026, kim2026, brini2026,
cherif2026}, and the mean scaled score is recommended in print on intermittent data \citep{corani2026}. On GitHub stars a live benchmark on the 400 most-starred repositories finds the typical series favoring the foundation models \citep{garza2026}, where the population sample of Study 1 finds the opposite \citep{wen2026}. A later test window on
familiar entities does not establish generalization to unseen ones \citep{moghadasi2026}, later windows of
known series leak through temporal dependence \citep{qiao2026}, pre-training contamination is now audited
directly \citep{li2026}, and earlier items in the protocol's lineage bear on the same ground
\citep{tsantilis2025, zodiac2026, meyer2025}.

\paragraph{Registered evaluation.} Replication has long been asked of forecasting papers \citep{hyndman2010},
and machine learning has a precedent for reviewing a protocol before its results \citep{preregister2021}.
This paper's protocol is a committed file whose registered bytes are byte prefixes of the file as it stands,
checked by each run's gate, and every change since is a dated addition that says what changed, when, and
whether it was decided before any target of the study it touches was read (Table~\ref{tab:ledger}).
