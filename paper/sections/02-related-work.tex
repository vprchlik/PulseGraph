\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Intermittent-demand methods and count models.} Series with many zero periods have been treated as
intermittent demand since \citet{croston1972}, who smoothed the demand size and the interval between demands
separately. The bias correction of \citet{syntetos2005} and the probability-updating variant of
\citet{teunter2011}, which smooths the occurrence indicator on every period, are the methods a reader in the
field looks for first. However, \citet{shenstone2005} showed that no stochastic model consistent with real
intermittent data underlies Croston's method, so the Croston line gives point forecasts without a predictive
law of its own.

Full laws come from count models. \citet{snyder2012} compared a Poisson benchmark, a negative binomial and a
hurdle shifted Poisson that keeps Croston's Bernoulli occurrence, all with dynamic parameters.
\citet{svetunkov2023} wrote occurrence and size as one state space model, iETS, whose occurrence equations
contain TSB's and Croston's as cases. Temporal aggregation \citep{nikolopoulos2011}, Tweedie laws
\citep{damato2025}, benchmark suites of distributional methods \citep{damato2026} and pooled hurdles with
forgetting \citep{bai2026} extend the line.

For its baselines the protocol registered classical members of that line. The count hurdle is a hurdle shifted
Poisson with a fixed-window occurrence estimate, and the empirical hurdle the same occurrence with the
empirical law of the recent positive days. Last, the dynamic count is a discounted Bayesian analogue of TSB.
The protocol excludes Croston and TSB by name without a reason. Neither carries a predictive law of its own,
and the dynamic count is read here as playing TSB's part.

\paragraph{Count scores and quantile scores on discrete data.} \citet{czado2009} is the standard reference for
assessing count forecasts by proper scoring rules and the randomized probability integral transform.
\citet{kolassa2016} argued that low retail counts need full discrete laws and scores that respect them, and
\citet{wang2024} scored intermittent-demand combinations with the log score, the discrete ranked probability
score and a Brier score. Quantile scores are proper for the quantiles they read \citep{gneiting2007} and equal
a weighted interval score at matching levels \citep{bracher2021}. The M5 uncertainty competition asked for
nine levels from 0.005 to 0.995 \citep{makridakis2022}, and \citet{ziel2022} argued that its leading methods
failed the data's overdispersion and frequent zeros.

Two recent evaluations drop every quantile level below the median because the forecasts there are zero for
every method \citep{damato2025, damato2026}. Even the discrete ranked probability score separates models
poorly on ultra-slow series \citep{tichy2025}. The property observed here on the very sparse series follows
from the definition. A quantile score reads only the scored quantiles, so two forecasts whose deciles agree
score the same for every target, and a count law with at least nine tenths of its mass at zero has all nine
deciles at zero. \qs{}, the registered primary, has that property, but the occurrence Brier score and the
discrete CRPS, the declared secondaries, do not.

\paragraph{Foundation models on intermittent data.} Chronos \citep{ansari2024, chronos2-2025} and TimesFM
\citep{das2023}, the two families run here frozen, and Moirai \citep{woo2024} are pretrained forecasters.
Moirai's output is a mixture with a negative-binomial component, where Chronos-2 and TimesFM expose quantiles.
Neither family's paper reports a count analysis, and the general benchmarks name no intermittent category
\citep{aksu2024, fevbench2026}.

Evaluations of frozen models on sparse counts are recent. One evaluates twelve models on five sparse panels by
event ranking, without a proper score \citep{schoess2026}. A benchmark of 38 methods sets Croston, SBA, TSB
and ADIDA beside Chronos and TimesFM under point and service measures \citep{chin2026}. TimesFM and
Chronos-Bolt meet Croston and TSB on quick-commerce demand \citep{kim2026}, and TimesFM 3 is tested on
simulated intermittent counts under a protocol frozen before any forecast \citep{ebrahim2026,
ebrahim2026zenodo}. Calibration, blind-spot and trained-hurdle studies complete the list \citep{adler2025,
ghoroghchian2026, musat2026}. No evaluation was found that wraps a frozen model's output in a hurdle under a
proper score. The nearest, the hurdle on frozen Chronos-2 output in \citet{chin2026}, is judged by point and
service measures.

Aggregate and per-series rankings of zero-shot models are known to disagree \citep{islam2026, kim2026,
brini2026, cherif2026}, and the mean scaled score is recommended in print on intermittent data
\citep{corani2026}. On GitHub stars a live benchmark on the 400 most-starred repositories finds the typical
series favoring the foundation models \citep{garza2026}. In contrast, the population sample of Study 1 finds
the opposite \citep{wen2026}.

A later test window on familiar entities does not establish generalization to unseen ones
\citep{moghadasi2026}, and later windows of known series leak through temporal dependence \citep{qiao2026}.
Pre-training contamination is now audited directly \citep{li2026}, and earlier items in the protocol's lineage
bear on the same ground \citep{tsantilis2025, zodiac2026, meyer2025}.

\paragraph{Registered evaluation.} Replication has long been asked of forecasting papers \citep{hyndman2010},
and machine learning has a precedent for reviewing a protocol before its results \citep{preregister2021}. Here
the protocol is a committed file whose registered bytes are byte prefixes of the file as it stands, checked by
each run's gate. Every change since is a dated addition that says what changed, when, and whether it was
decided before any target of the study it touches was read (Table~\ref{tab:ledger}).
