\section{Design}
\label{sec:design}

\paragraph{The forecasters.} A daily count forecast has two parts, whether anything happens and how much
happens when it does, and the forecasters differ in where each part comes from (Figure~\ref{fig:design}). The
three foundation models are frozen Chronos-2, TimesFM 3 and TimesFM 2.5 at revisions pinned on 2026-09-12;
their native output is nine deciles of a continuous forecast, clipped at zero and with crossings repaired,
which are the \emph{raw} variant. The \emph{completed} control turns the nine deciles into a count law $F$ by
a fixed rule and takes both parts from the model. The \emph{hurdle} keeps the model's positive law and
replaces its zero mass with an occurrence probability estimated from the context alone: the 28-day Jeffreys
estimate $p = (n_{+} + 1/2) / 29$ in Study 2 (the fixed hurdle), and in Study 3 the discounted Beta-Bernoulli
estimate of the registered dynamic count (the dynamic hurdle). The fourth cell of that two-by-two, the
model's own occurrence $1 - F(0)$ with a count positive law, was registered for Study 3 as a declared
secondary and is computed post hoc on npm and the grid. The count baselines take both parts from the context:
the count hurdle \hc{}, a hurdle shifted Poisson fitted to the positive days of the last 28 with the same
Jeffreys occurrence \citep{snyder2012}; the empirical hurdle, the same occurrence with the empirical law of
those days, declared secondary; and the dynamic count, a Beta-Bernoulli occurrence and a Gamma-Poisson size
forgotten at 0.98 a day \citep{teunter2011}. Two references from Study 1 complete the set, \lm{} and
\aets{}. The post-hoc TSB hurdle is the count hurdle with TSB's two estimates in place of the 28-day ones.

\paragraph{The completion and its floor.} From the nine repaired deciles $q_{0.1}, \dots, q_{0.9}$ the
quantile function is the linear interpolation through $(0, 0)$ and the nine knots for $u \le 0.9$ and the
exponential tail $Q(u) = q_{0.9} + s \log(0.1 / (1 - u))$ for $u > 0.9$ with scale
$s = \max(1, q_{0.9} - q_{0.8})$; the count is $Y = \max(0, \lfloor Q(U) + 0.5 \rfloor)$, and $F$ is its
right-continuous CDF (Appendix~\ref{app:s2design}). The hurdle's positive law
$G_+(k) = [F(k) - F(0)] / [1 - F(0)]$ is this marginal law truncated at one, not a size forecast the model
produced for positive days. The completion has a floor. When every repaired decile is below 0.5, the
interpolated part of $Q$ rounds to zero and only the tail can produce a positive count, so
\[
P(Y > 0) = P\bigl(Q(U) \ge 0.5\bigr) = 0.1\, e^{-(0.5 - q_{0.9})},
\]
which lies between $0.1 e^{-0.5} = 0.0607$ and $0.1$ whatever the model forecast (Figure~\ref{fig:floor}).
On a series that is zero nine days in ten the ``model's own zero mass'' is therefore set by this rule.
Counted after the run from the saved laws of the 300 NYC 311 checkpoints, descriptive and post hoc: of the
600 completed forecasts of each model in the very sparse stratum, 580, 544 and 588 sit on the floor for
Chronos-2, TimesFM 3 and TimesFM 2.5, with a median occurrence probability of 0.0607 to 0.0609 against 0.0172
for the dynamic hurdle on the same windows; in the sparse stratum 150, 62 and 230 of 600 do, and in the
dense stratum 2, 0 and 0.

\paragraph{The scores.} The registered primary of all three studies is \qs{}, twice the mean pinball loss
over the nine deciles $\tau \in \{0.1, \dots, 0.9\}$ and the $h$ target days,
$\qs{} = \frac{2}{9h} \sum_{t} \sum_{\tau} \max\{\tau (y_t - q_{t,\tau}), (\tau - 1)(y_t - q_{t,\tau})\}$,
a proper score for the nine quantiles \citep{gneiting2007}, chosen because the models' native output is nine
quantiles and the three studies were to be comparable. It reads nine integer deciles of a count law, so an
occurrence probability that moves by less than one decile leaves it unchanged, and two forecasts whose
deciles coincide in every window score identically whatever the target. Two declared secondaries do not share
that blindness: the occurrence Brier score $(p - \mathbf{1}[y > 0])^2$, with $p$ the forecast's probability
of a positive day, and the discrete CRPS $\sum_{k \ge 0} (F(k) - \mathbf{1}[y \le k])^2$ \citep{czado2009},
recorded as unavailable for the one npm series whose counts defeat its certified bound. The paper shows both
beside \qs{} for every contrast of the occurrence question and reads them as the declared secondaries they
are. Two conventions hold throughout: differences are variant minus reference, so a negative number favors
the variant; and a table or a row marked secondary or post hoc adds no test, while a median of exactly zero
has no sign.

\paragraph{The panels, the calendar and the inference.} Table~\ref{tab:panels} gives the four panels, each
natural one sampled from an enumerated frame with no popularity filter (Appendices~\ref{app:design},
\ref{app:s2panel} and~\ref{app:s3detail}). The npm and NYC 311 strata are the context zero fraction over the
90 days before the first cutoff, $[0, 0.5)$ dense, $[0.5, 0.9)$ sparse and $[0.9, 1]$ very sparse, with the
first 100 complete series of each stratum taken and no transfer of slots; npm's very sparse stratum holds the
9 series that qualified. The grid's positive law is the count hurdle's own family, and its oracle is a
diagnostic, not a competitor. Every model sees only a series' own history through the cutoff. Window scores
are averaged over origins within series and horizon, series weigh equally within a panel or a stratum, and
each registered comparison reports the mean paired difference with a pointwise 95 percent percentile
interval from 20,000 bootstrap replicates of whole series, the median difference, the series lower, tied and
higher, and a two-sided approximate Wilcoxon signed-rank test that omits zero differences, with one
Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment at $q = 0.05$ over each family. The mean and the typical series are always shown together, and the series that carries a mean is named.
The registered direction of a test is the median's sign, and a median of exactly zero has none; the side the
rank sums favor, where reported, is a reading the registration does not make.
